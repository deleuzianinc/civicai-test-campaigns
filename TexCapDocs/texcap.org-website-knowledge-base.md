## Crafting non-partisan policy solutions for lawmakers at the State and Local level.

At **TexCap Policy Institute**(TXCPI), we’re crafting policy solutions from leading private sector leaders, lawmakers, and researchers to help policy makers at the State and Local level craft solutions backed by data. We aren’t a think-tank that operates in partisan silos but rather we foster ideas that work across aisles and across the State.

![](https://texcap.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/icons8-tax-641.png)

### Equitable Taxes

Fairer tax systems that prioritize responsible stewardship of taxpayer dollars and balance Texas’ growing needs

![](https://texcap.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/icons8-route-64.png)

### **Infrastructure Investment**

Increasing access to rural and urban broadband and expanding smarter multi-modal transportation systems

![](https://texcap.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/icons8-nuclear-64.png)

# About Us

## TexCap Policy Institute is a 501(c)3non-profit, non-partisan think-tank and policy institute

**Our Vision**

To ground innovation in **ethical stewardship** and **prosperity** through public policy at the State and Local level, increasing quality of life without partisan division.

**Our Mission**

To design and advocate for **_private-sector guided_**, **_public-interest policies_** that channel private capital into building long-term prosperity for all Texans through **infrastructure**, **equitable tax structures**, and **responsible governance** rooted in transparency, innovation, and impact.

# Programs

[![](https://i0.wp.com/texcap.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/1.png?fit=990%2C558&ssl=1)](https://texcap.org/initiatives/chief-of-staff-academy/)

The Chief of Staff Academy trains two cohorts of 10 current and prospective in the Spring and Fall. Each participant completes a 9-week curriculum on leadership, budgeting, crisis management, and strategy. Each member of the program is award a “Chief of Staff Certification” that let’s their member, constituents, and the public know that they have training and experience to run their office with expertise.

[![](https://i0.wp.com/texcap.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/4-1.png?fit=990%2C558&ssl=1)](https://texcap.org/initiatives/water-watts/)

The Water & Watts program focuses on securing Texas’ natural resilience through smarter energy and water systems. The Water & Watts Program focuses on the twin pillars of Texas’ physical infrastructure: energy and water. From modernizing the grid to expanding reuse and storage, the program identifies practical, technology-driven solutions that strengthen reliability while reducing waste.

[![](https://i0.wp.com/texcap.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/2.png?fit=990%2C558&ssl=1)](https://texcap.org/initiatives/the-prosperity-project/)

The Prosperity Project reimagines economic development for the 21st century emphasizing speed, transparency, and stewardship. The program advances reforms to make Texas the easiest state to build, innovate, and invest responsibly. Its priorities include permitting modernization, performance-based incentives, and better data on local ROI.

[![](https://i0.wp.com/texcap.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/5.png?fit=990%2C558&ssl=1)](https://texcap.org/initiatives/lone-star-link-up/)

The Lone Star Link-Up strengthens the connective tissue of the Texas economy — roads, ports, and broadband. The program promotes smart, regionally coordinated investments that link rural and urban Texas, ensuring every community can participate in growth.

[![](https://i0.wp.com/texcap.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/3.png?fit=990%2C558&ssl=1)](https://texcap.org/initiatives/nextgov-texas/)

NextGov Texas accelerates digital transformation across state and local agencies — from procurement reform to AI readiness and cybersecurity. The program helps policymakers and public administrators adopt tools that make government faster, more transparent, and more responsive to citizens

# Chief Of Staff Academy

![](https://i0.wp.com/texcap.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/1.png?resize=990%2C558&ssl=1)

The **Chief of Staff Academy** is a professional, nonpartisan development program designed to equip rising and current public servants with the leadership, management, and strategic skills needed to run high-performing offices in the Texas Legislature and local government.

Each year, the Academy trains two cohorts of ten participants — one in the Spring and one in the Fall. Over a nine-week curriculum, participants engage in intensive, practice-based instruction led by experienced Chiefs of Staff and policy experts.

Graduates of the program earn a **Chief of Staff Certification**, signaling to elected officials, constituents, and the public that they possess the expertise and ethical grounding required to lead effectively in the public sector.

**Program Overview:**

Participants learn to manage policy priorities, navigate political dynamics, and uphold the highest standards of public service. Coursework combines real-world case studies, mentorship, and peer collaboration to develop operational and strategic mastery.

**Example Curriculum (Spring 2026 Cohort):**

- **Week 1**: Foundations: The Role of a Chief of Staff
- **Week 2:** Policy & Power Part I: How to Shape Legislative Impact for your district
- **Week 3:** Policy & Power Part II: How to Shape Legislative Impact for your district
- **Week 4**: Communications, Crisis Management & Constituent Relations
- **Week 5:** Managing People & Office Culture
- **Week 6:** Strategy, Time, and Political Navigation
- **Week 7:** Budgeting & Administrative Systems
- **Week 8:** Leadership and Mentorship
- **Week 9**: Capstone Session – Final Class and Next Steps

Applications for the first cohort will open in December 2025. Those selected for the inaugural cohort will be announced in January of 2026.

# Lone Star Link Up

## **Connecting Every Corner of Texas**

The **Lone Star Link Up Program** focuses on the connective infrastructure that drives Texas’ economy — roads, ports, broadband, and transmission. Designed for regional planners, economic developers, and policymakers, the program advances strategies that link rural and urban communities, ensuring that growth and opportunity reach every part of the state.

By promoting regional coordination, public-private partnerships, and technology-driven planning, Lone Star Link Up helps Texas remain the most dynamic, connected, and competitive state in America.

**Program Focus Areas:**

- **Regional Coordination:** Encouraging collaboration among cities, counties, utilities, and economic development districts to replace siloed infrastructure planning.
- **Broadband Expansion:** Accelerating universal access to high-speed internet so every Texan can participate in the digital economy.
- **Transportation & Logistics:** Supporting multimodal investments — highways, rail, and ports — that reduce congestion and improve trade flow.

**Ongoing Research Initiatives:**

- _Study on Potential Cost Savings by Implementing a High-Speed Rail System_

The **Lone Star Link Up Program** embodies TexCap’s commitment to growth — connecting communities, strengthening supply chains, and ensuring that the next generation of Texas prosperity is shared from the Gulf Coast to the Panhandle.

# NextGov Texas

## **Building a Smarter, Faster, and More Transparent State**

The **NextGov Texas Program** accelerates digital transformation across state and local government — helping public institutions embrace technology that improves efficiency, transparency, and public trust. Designed for policymakers, administrators, and civic innovators, the program examines how Texas can modernize governance by integrating ethical AI, cybersecurity, and smarter procurement systems into everyday operations.

By connecting Austin’s innovation ecosystem with the needs of government, NextGov Texas builds a bridge between the private sector’s agility and the public sector’s responsibility — ensuring Texans receive the best of both worlds.

**Program Focus Areas:**

- **Procurement Reform:** Streamlining acquisition processes to make it easier for cities and agencies to adopt innovative, cost-saving tools.
- **Cybersecurity & Data Privacy:** Establishing baseline competencies for every public institution to safeguard citizen data and digital infrastructure.
- **Artificial Intelligence & Automation:** Leveraging emerging technologies to improve transparency, responsiveness, and efficiency while upholding strong ethical standards.
- **Digital Governance:** Promoting interoperability, data-sharing, and modern platforms that strengthen accountability and reduce waste.

**Ongoing Research Initiatives:**

- _AI and Data Ethics Standards for Public Agencies_
- _Cybersecurity Checklist for State & Local Governments_

NextGov Texas advances TexCap’s vision for a **“Smart State”** — one where technology and accountability work hand in hand to deliver better outcomes for taxpayers and rebuild public confidence in government.

# The Prosperity Project

## **Redefining Economic Growth for the 21st Century**

The **Prosperity Project** reimagines how Texas builds, invests, and grows — focusing on speed, transparency, and stewardship in economic development. This program equips policymakers, entrepreneurs, and local leaders with the tools to create a more agile and accountable economy where innovation and productivity drive shared prosperity.

By bridging the gap between private enterprise and public purpose, the Prosperity Project helps Texas remain the best place to do business — and the fairest place to build a future. Participants explore how smarter regulation, performance-based incentives, and global competitiveness can shape a next-generation economic framework that benefits all Texans.

**Program Focus Areas:**

- **Performance-Based Incentives:** Rewarding transparency, productivity, and long-term community value instead of short-term transactions.

- _Permitting Reform: How Long to Develop Housing in Each Major Metro?_

The **Prosperity Project** advances TexCap’s mission to design and advocate for private-sector-guided, public-interest policies — ensuring that growth in Texas remains both competitive and equitable for the decades ahead.

# Water & Watts

## **Securing Texas’ Natural Resilience**

The **Water & Watts Program** explores the intersection of Texas’ two most vital resources (water and energy) and develops policy solutions that strengthen the state’s long-term resilience. Designed for policymakers, industry leaders, and researchers, the program combines applied research, stakeholder engagement, and data-driven analysis to identify scalable infrastructure reforms that protect both consumers without sacrificing private sector innovation.

Participants and contributors work to advance a more reliable, efficient, and sustainable foundation for Texas’ future by examining how modern technology, market innovation, and regional collaboration can secure the systems that power and sustain our communities.

**Program Focus Areas:**

- **Grid Modernization & Distributed Energy:** Evaluating how microgrids, distributed generation, and energy diversification can enhance reliability and national security.
- **Water Reuse & Storage:** Assessing technologies and governance models that expand reuse, improve efficiency, and ensure water access during drought and growth cycles.
- **Regional Integration:** Exploring how water and power systems can be better connected to minimize waste, increase resilience, and support statewide development.
- **Technology & Data:** Promoting the adoption of advanced metering, AI, and predictive modeling tools to modernize water and energy management.

**Ongoing Research Initiatives:**

- _Water Entity Consolidation & Regional Governance Study_
- _Texas Grid Modernization & Distributed Energy Framework_
- _Data Centers & the Texas Grid_

The **Water & Watts Program** is rooted in TexCap’s belief that resilience is not built through regulation alone but through the intelligent alignment of technology, markets, and public purpose.

# Newly Founded Think Tank Unveils 89th Legislature Analysis, Blueprint for a New Texas Economy

June 25, 2025

—

2–3 minutes

Categories: [press release](https://texcap.org/category/press-releases/)

**AUSTIN, TX**  — The TexCap Policy Institute, a newly founded non-partisan think tank devoted to data driven, _private-sector guided_ _, public-interest policies_, has announced the release of its 89th Legislative Session Report: A 30+ page report that evaluates the Texas Legislature’s impact on Taxes, Infrastructure, Water & Natural Resources, and Technology.

“Texas has grown at historic speeds over the past decade but that doesn’t come without consequence,” said Gavin Nicholson, Founder and Chief Executive Officer of TexCap Policy Institute. “We are seeing record-breaking profits from corporations, innovation that rivals our most technologically advanced neighbors, and opportunities that come once in a lifetime.”

“At the same time, we’re seeing unrelenting property taxes, pressing challenges surrounding the stewardship of our natural resources, and the need to create an economy for the next generation of Texans. **We don’t have to accept this dichotomy as the norm.**”

The inaugural report identifies issues the legislature must solve and potential solutions to those problems. Those proposed solutions, summarized, are:

- **Reduce** property tax burdens on homeowners through interim studies on Franchise tax, Land Value Tax, and Split-Rate Property Tax
- **Continue** to support and invest in broadband development while repealing anti-high-speed transit policies
- **Increase** State funding for Nuclear, Geothermal, and Hydrogen development and  investment in regional Water Development Partnerships
- **Continue** supporting AI regulatory (HB149) while requiring datacenter operators to produce their own energy to offset grid strain

The think tank’s initial board is governed by individuals with deep public and private-sector experience who will advise on policy recommendations. They include individuals in the Venture Capital, Water, Energy, Tech, and Economic Development.

**Omar Zaki, MBA**: Managing Director at Qima Ventures, Investor Relations of Q-Branch

**Ashley Myers**: Executive Director of Texas Association of Water Companies, Director at Whitmire & Munoz LLC

**Zhelun Chen**: Government Affairs Professional

**Joshua Lapidus**: Chief Executive Officer and Founder of Azos Labs

During the interim, the think tank will focus on creating resources for State and Local lawmakers, whitepapers outlining innovative policy ideals, and advocating for policies that align with their upcoming initiatives.

**“Lawmakers and their staff need a non-partisan think tank dedicated to solving the complex and convoluted policy issues that arise before each session,” said Gavin Nicholson. “We can’t build good policy the day we need it.”**

To view a copy of the full report, please visit [https://texcap.org/2025/06/26/89th-session-report/](https://texcap.org/2025/06/26/89th-session-report/)

###

_The TexCap Policy Institute was founded in June 2025 in Austin, Texas. Focusing on crafting non-partisan policy solutions for State and Local lawmakers, they design and advocate for private-sector guided, public-interest policies that channel private capital into building long-term prosperity for all Texans through infrastructure, equitable tax structures, and responsible governance rooted in transparency, innovation, and impact._

# Startups ≠ Small Businesses: A Policy Blueprint for Texas to Unlock High-Growth Capital

July 8, 2025

—

3–5 minutes

Categories: [Government Innovation](https://texcap.org/category/white-paper/government-innovation/), [NextGov Texas](https://texcap.org/category/programs/nextgov-texas/), [Prosperity Project](https://texcap.org/category/programs/prosperity-project/), [white paper](https://texcap.org/category/white-paper/)

## **Why Texas Must Distinguish Startups from Small Businesses, And Stop Funding the Wrong Econom** y

Texas prides itself on independence, scale, and speed. But in one of the most crucial economic development decisions of the 21st century, the state lags woefully behind: it still treats startups as small businesses.

Let’s call this what it is, an antiquated, bureaucratic mistake that threatens Texas’ role as the epicenter of American innovation. While Texas Economic Development and Tourism, the Texas Workforce Commission, and countless regional chambers obsess over Main Street retention and small business subsidies, the real drivers of job creation, research commercialization, and frontier technology, **_the startups, remain underfunded, misunderstood, and effectively invisible in the eyes of the law._**

According to the Kauffman Foundation, net new firms, specifically high-growth startups and not new existing business types, are responsible for nearly all net new job creation in the U.S. Yet state-level incentives, tax structures, and even franchise regulations prioritize brick-and-mortar establishments over scalable ventures. Why? Because we’ve been relying on federal templates and SBA-style frameworks designed in 1953, before the term “startup” even existed.

That’s like regulating autonomous vehicles with 1950s traffic law.

### **The Cost of Conflating Startups with Small Businesses**

A startup, by Steve Blank’s seminal definition, is “a temporary organization in search of a repeatable and scalable business model.” A small business, by contrast, is a known quantity, often locally owned, cash-flow optimized, stable, and intentionally limited in scale. One is a scientific experiment; the other is a family store.

Yet in Texas, our funding mechanisms, franchise tax codes, grant criteria, and economic development incentives treat them as one and the same. This misalignment means Texas cities are trying to grow high-value innovation economies while measuring ROI in sandwich shops and signage improvements.

And it gets worse.

Many of the state’s tax policies, from franchise exemptions to ad valorem breaks, were designed with fixed-asset, location-dependent companies in mind. Startups, especially in tech, life sciences, and advanced energy, operate without such assets in early stages. So we’re incentivizing the companies that can already survive, while ignoring the ventures that actually need catalytic support.

If you think this is harmless semantics, take a look at TexCap Policy Institute’s 89th Legislative Report: in a session that passed over 1,200 bills, not one established a statewide definition of a “startup.” Innovation was thrown in with “government efficiency,” “blockchain pilot programs,” and vague “JETI” incentives, none of which directly address startup capital formation or commercialization.

### **The Franchise Tax is Outdated for the Innovation Economy**

According to the Texas Comptroller’s data, the franchise tax threshold has ballooned to $2.47 million in revenue, meaning many of Texas’ highest-growth ventures are exempt from the tax entirely. While that sounds entrepreneur-friendly, it creates a revenue hole for the state and de-incentivizes scale: when you finally start to grow, the state hits you with retroactive burdens.

More importantly, startups that raise capital, rather than earn it through revenue, get left out of financial incentive models altogether. Texas handicaps frontier companies if our own fiscal design penalizes ambition.

### **A Call for a Dedicated Office of Startups and Innovation**

In drawing from my encouragement of the Federal Government, I’d like to push Texas to lead by example and be the first state in the country pushing forward the distinction of startups from other new businesses.  How? A dedicated Office of Startups and Innovation, distinct from our small business and tourism-focused agencies. This office would help:

- **Define startups in statute**, separating them from traditional small businesses in all tax, grant, and investment language.

- **Coordinate regional venture development**, in partnership with incubators, venture studios, accelerators, universities, and corporate labs, aligned with economic development offices and regions.

- **Create co-investment vehicles**, like Israel’s Yozma or British Patient Capital, channeling private dollars through matched public commitments appropriate to supporting startups – not direct investment in such high risk ventures, but in support of Startup Development Organizations and reducing the costs of infrastructure or regulations that create barriers that ntrepreneurs have to overcome merely to try..

- **Establish startup focused curriculum,** distinct from small business programs or the generic entrepreneurship classes available in high schools, universities, and private sector programs, Texas can lead educating people in startups – both as would be founders and potential investors.

**Startup founders in Texas don’t need handouts. They need recognition, fast-track access, and policy infrastructure that understands what they’re building. And frankly, so does our state.**

# “@grok is this true?”A Policy Primer on Artificial Intelligence (AI) Policy in Texas

August 26, 2025

—

2–3 minutes

Categories: [Government Innovation](https://texcap.org/category/white-paper/government-innovation/), [Lone Star Link Up](https://texcap.org/category/programs/lone-star-link-up/), [NextGov Texas](https://texcap.org/category/programs/nextgov-texas/), [Water & Watts](https://texcap.org/category/programs/water-watts/), [white paper](https://texcap.org/category/white-paper/)

**At the TexCap Policy Institute, we believe Texas must take a pragmatic, nonpartisan approach to artificial intelligence.** Policy and regulations should encourage innovation while ensuring accountability, transparency, and consumer protection. We seek to ground the debate around AI in facts, not hype or snappy quips.

This primer demonstrates how Texas can harness AI’s strengths while mitigating its risks. Texans deserve policies that are both forward-looking and firmly rooted in responsible governance that are free of outside influence seeking to misrepresent the good and the bad.

Our sources will pull data from leading academics and researchers in the Artificial Intelligence space. This includes publications that may not be from the typical “academic” background but still have a wealth of knowledge to offer in this space. To determine certain variables, we had to make certain assumptions that may not be publicly available. These assumptions are stated before they are made to inform the reader of what they are: Guesses based on the best publicly available information.

We will be using examples of AI in pop culture, academics, and current deployments of AI. This will include both good use cases and bad use cases that policy makers must confront.

AI is not new. It has been around in its most rudimentary forms since the 1950s. We have lived with AI for decades and cannot let false moral panic cloud our judgement of reality. We hope this policy primer answers those questions and provides a sense of how we can continue to work with this technology and not against it.

Key Findings you will learn from this primer:

1. AI is not conscious, not 100% accurate, and contains user/developer bias
2. One Hyperscale Data Center, the largest type of data center, could use 0.002% of Texas’ water
3. One Hyperscale Data Center could use 0.2% of GWh demand on Texas’ grid
4. 1 ChatGPT Query is estimated to use 7.59mL of water and 0.3 watt-hours of energy in Texas
5. Autonomous Vehicles are at least 57.65% less likely to get involved in a crash compared to a human driver.
6. Most AI policy at the Local level is preempted by the State
7. Over 51% of Federal, State, and Local governments staff are using AI already

# Environmental Risk to Baseload Energy: How Texas Could Retrofit Zombie Wells into Closed-Loop Geothermal Systems

October 20, 2025

—

1–2 minutes

Categories: [Water & Watts](https://texcap.org/category/programs/water-watts/), [white paper](https://texcap.org/category/white-paper/)

Texas has more than 8,000 idle or orphaned oil wells. “Zombie Wells” that leak methane, that threaten groundwater, and impose more than $1 billion in plugging liabilities on operators and taxpayers. Recent advances in closed-loop geothermal technology now make it technically feasible to retrofit many of these wells into sealed heat-exchange systems capable of generating base-load, carbon-free electricity. This white paper quantifies the economic upside, outlines regulatory and financing barriers, and offers a step-by-step policy roadmap to accelerate pilot projects and scaled deployments across the state.

This white paper outlines a roadmap for policy and regulatory reform:

1. How to identify “geothermal‑ready” idle wells using depth, casing‑integrity, and formation‑temperature criteria that can be pulled directly from Railroad Commission data,
2. Step‑by‑step permit pathway from site screening to consolidated Railroad Commission approval that can shorten retrofit timelines to 90 days; and
3. Demonstrating how redirecting up 80 percent of a plugging bond toward retrofit Capital Expenditure (CAPEX) can turn a liability into a positive‑NPV project for operators

Read the white paper below.

**# PRIVACY POLICY**

**Last updated June 04, 2025**

This Privacy Notice for TexCap Policy Institute (“ **we**,” “ **us**,” or “ **our**“), describes how and why we might access, collect, store, use, and/or share (“ **process**“) your personal information when you use our services (“ **Services**“), including when you:

**Questions or concerns?** Reading this Privacy Notice will help you understand your privacy rights and choices. **Do we process any sensitive personal information?** Some of the information may be considered “special” or “sensitive” in certain jurisdictions, for example your racial or ethnic origins, sexual orientation, and religious beliefs. We do not process sensitive personal information.

**Do we collect any information from third parties?** We do not collect any information from third parties.

**How do we process your information?** We process your information to provide, improve, and administer our Services, communicate with you, for security and fraud prevention, and to comply with law. Learn more about [how we process your information](https://texcap.org/privacy-policy/#infouse).

**In what situations and with which parties do we share personal information?** We may share information in specific situations and with specific third parties. Learn more about [when and with whom we share your personal information](https://texcap.org/privacy-policy/#whoshare).

**What are your rights?** Depending on where you are located geographically, the applicable privacy law may mean you have certain rights regarding your personal information. Learn more about [your privacy rights](https://texcap.org/privacy-policy/#privacyrights).

Want to learn more about what we do with any information we collect? [Review the Privacy Notice in full](https://texcap.org/privacy-policy/#toc).

# 89TH LEGISLATIVE SESSION AT A GLANCE

The $8 9 ^ { \\mathrm { t h } }$ Texas Legislature continued a trend of filing an increased number of bills in both Chambers with over 9,014 bills and joint resolutions filed. This is alarming due to the often duplicative nature of bills. If the Texas legislature were to hear every single bill on its merits, there would need to be at least 64 bills that would need to "move" at some stage in the process for all 140 days straight. This is unsustainable and can create a bad policy environment due to the excessive number of filed bills. For comparison, in the $8 4 ^ { \\mathrm { t h } }$ (2015) legislative session, only 6,476 bills and joint resolutions were filed.

Lawmakers should consider looking at other states that have adopted "bill restrictions" in their respective chambers such as California, Colorado, Florida, North Carolina, and Tennessee. This would allow for more streamlined collaboration between respective parties and reduce the number of "symbolic" bills which serve no purpose other than to take up valuable time. "The idea is not to restrict lawmakers' work, but to reduce the amount of time spent on superfluous proposals and to allow more time for substantive legislation."v

# EQUITABLE TAX STRUCTURES

The Texas Legislature provided $$ 10$ billion in new funding for property tax relief, increased homestead exemptions, and sent numerous ad valorem tax exemptions to the voters this November. Notably, the Texas Legislature did not pass more regressive taxingvi policies such as a ban on property taxes which would have led to an increased sales tax.

It is obvious among Republican and Democratic Lawmakers that the property tax system in its current state is not sustainable. According to a Hobby School of Public Affairs at the University of Houston poll, $50 %$ of Texans wanted lawmakers to prioritize property tax relief.vii The issue, however, is that the property tax system is not one that incentivizes or rewards good-faith actors. Homeowners and property owners are punished under the current system when they improve their property. Lower-income Texans are forced out of neighborhoods when development in their neighborhoods occurs due to the rise in property values which equates to higher taxes.

If the legislature intends to keep its property tax system, it should continue to explore successful ideas like a circuit break for all Texans or appraisal caps for certain "legacy" homeowners (people who have owned their home for at least ${ \\mathsf { 1 0 + } }$ years). Unfortunately, continuing to "buy down" property taxes and offering exemptions will not offer long-term relief as they do not address the underlying causes for the current juxtaposition many local governments are facing: Residents who want increased property values without the higher property tax values.

There also seems to be a disconnect of what type of districts are increasing their burden on homeowners. While some media outletsviii place the blame largely on School Districts, data from the Comptroller's office suggests otherwise. Special Districts and County taxes had the highest average annual percentage increase when local jurisdictions levied property taxes. The legislature can, and should, continue to fund a higher M&O obligation with the State budget to offset the charge at the local level.

The legislature went into session knowing that the demand for the grid would increase in the summer and the winter, partially due to the increase in data centers moving to Texas, the increase in population, and increasingly more extreme weather conditions. Some legislators went into this session with a plan to work with Retail Electric Providers (REP), Transmission and Distribution Service Providers (TDU/TDSP), and Operators (OP) to provide resiliency to our grid to avoid issues like many Texans encountered during Winter Storm Uri in 2021.

"We are blessed to be a state that has experienced continual growth for decades. This is also true of our electric usage and demand. # Zombie Wells in Texas

The issue of zombie wells (abandoned improperly plugged oil and gas wells) was a growing concern this session. The Texas Railroad Commission has received $$ 79,673,757$ in federal funding to plug wells.xviiiThese wells pose serious risks to public health, groundwater safety, and land value across both urban and rural Texas. While the Texas Railroad Commission has received federal funds to plug orphaned wells, questions remain about the pace of remediation, the sufficiency of bonding requirements, and the transparency of enforcement.xix Lawmakers from both parties agreed that private operators should be held responsible for their environmental liabilities, but a durable solution requires sustained investment, smarter tracking, and stronger accountability to avoid passing this billion-dollar burden to taxpayers.

# Water use and infrastructure

In a state as large and drought-prone as Texas, water policy remains a quiet but existential issue. Legislators acknowledged that population growth, industrial expansion, and extreme weather are stressing the state's already stretched water systems. Rural areas face aquifer depletion, while urban and suburban regions struggle with aging infrastructure and flood resilience.

Despite a broad consensus on the need for long-term investment, progress was uneven partly due to political fatigue and partly due to a lack of coordinated water governance. An overwhelming number of Texans, $56 %$ , do not think the legislature is spending enough on water.xx This holds true across partisan and geographic boundaries.

Without aggressive modernization of water infrastructure and continued coordination across all stakeholders, Texas risks undercutting its economic growth and quality of life in fast-growing regions.

# Notable Bills Vetoed by the Governor

House Bill 1690 by Representative Gerdes - Amends the Texas Water Code to require all groundwater conservation districts to adopt rules mandating notice to residents for applications to transfer groundwater out of the district.

The Governor vetoed this bill stating, "This bill would increase the regulatory hurdles to convey water resources. And it would do so needlessly. State law already authorizes groundwater conservation districts to adopt rules necessary to manage groundwater resources, including rules for public notice and hearings on groundwater permits."

We disagree with the Governor's analysis. When an application is submitted to transfer groundwater out of a district, community members and elected officials should receive notice to ensure they have an opportunity to voice concerns if the transfer is not appropriate. While Groundwater Conservation Districts already have meaningful power to deny permits, they are missing a formal process that allows affected communities to actively participate.

Water is a critical resource that cannot, and should not, be sold only to the highest bidder. There must be surgical calculations that weigh the long-term benefits and cost. Simply relying on profit margins is a disservice to the residents that live on the $70 %$ of Texas land that are Groundwater Conservation Districts.

# AI Regulation

Artificial Intelligence (AI) emerged as a policy frontier this session, with legislators grappling with how to balance innovation and oversight. As more state agencies and local governments adopt AI for service delivery, licensing, and enforcement, concerns grew over bias, transparency, and workforce displacement. # Environmental Risk to Baseload Energy: How Texas Could Retrofit Zombie Wells into Closed-Loop Geothermal Systems

Prepared by Gavin Nicholson Reviewed by Ken Wisian, Ph.D. OCTOBER 2025

# Executive Summary

Texas has more than 8,000 idle or orphaned oil wells, “zombie wells” that leak methane, that threaten groundwater, and impose more than $$ 1$ billion in plugging liabilities on operators and taxpayers. Recent advances in closed-loop geothermal technology now make it technically feasible to retrofit many of these wells into sealed heat-exchange systems capable of generating base-load, carbon-free electricity. This white paper quantifies the economic upside, outlines regulatory and financing barriers, and offers a step-by-step policy roadmap to accelerate pilot projects and scaled deployments across the state.

This white paper outlines a roadmap for policy and regulatory reform:

1. How to identify “geothermal‑ready” idle wells using depth, casing‑integrity, and formation‑temperature criteria that can be pulled directly from Railroad Commission data,
2. Step‑by‑step permit pathway from site screening to consolidated Railroad Commission approval that can shorten retrofit timelines to 90 days; and
3. Demonstrating how redirecting up 80 percent of a plugging bond toward retrofit Capital Expenditure (CAPEX) can turn a liability into a positive‑NPV project for operators.

# THE PROBLEM: COSTLY LIABILITIES BENEATH OUR FEET

Texas has long been the nation’s energy workhorse, drilling more than one million oil and gas wells since the Spindletop discovery. As fields mature, however, thousands of wells slip into the “orphan” status: wells that have been inactive, non-compliant for a minimum of 12 months and the operator has been delinquent for greater than 12 months:

~9,000 documented orphan wells;i plugging backlog grows ${ \\sim } 1 0 %$ per year. Methane leaks equal to an estimated 253.1 BcF annuallyii eroding Texas’ airquality goals. Plugging cost can soar up to $$ 76,000$ per well with some cases reaching $$ 1$ million.iii Rural landowners face depressed property values and groundwater risks; state regulators face mounting reclamation bills.

In short, the state faces a compounding environmental and fiscal liability. The cost of doing nothing is more methane in the atmosphere, more public dollars spent on remediation, and a missed chance to extract additional economic value from existing subsurface assets.

# TECHNOLOGY PRIMER: CLOSED-LOOP GEOTHERMAL SYSTEMS

Texas has long tapped subsurface resources for oil and gas; while still in the prototype stage, closedloop geothermal repurposes that expertise for heat instead of hydrocarbons. In a closed-loop system, a sealed working fluid circulates through a wellbore, absorbing heat from the surrounding formation before returning to the surface to drive a turbine. Because the loop is sealed and does not involve fracture creation, no produced water is brought to the surface, eliminating inducedseismicity and water-handling concerns that attach to conventional hydrothermal projects.

Recent pilot wells in Houstoniv and Californiav show that stable outlet temperatures of 140-180 $^ \\circ \\mathsf { C }$ were more than enough for binary-cycle power production while early cost projections suggest parity with new gas peakers once scale economies Page 2 of 5 \| [www.texcap.org](http://www.texcap.org/)

kick in. By leveraging existing wellbores, Texas can sidestep a major portion of drilling expenditures (CAPEX) and mitigate the environmental footprint associated with green field geothermal. In short, closed-loop technology translates the state’s drilling heritage into a next-generation baseload resource that is both dispatchable and emissionsfree.

Beyond these core advantages, several design variants allow closed-loop retrofits to match diverse well conditions:

Coaxial Tubing – A production string inside the existing casing forms a “tube-within-a-tube”; hot fluid rises in the inner tube while cooler fluid descends in the annulus, maximizing heat extraction without cementing new liners. This has the lowest thermal potential.

U-Tube or Multilateral Loops – Where sidetracks exist, a U-tube configuration further increases surface area and heat sweep, boosting output without additional drilling footage.

Downhole Heat Exchangers – In wells with excellent bottom-hole temperature but compromised casing, an entirely new sealed heat exchanger can be lowered, isolating the working fluid from corroded pipe.

Most retrofits will pair with binary-cycle Organic Rankine Engines, converting outlet fluid at $1 4 0 – 1 8 0 \ ^ { \\circ } \\mathsf { C }$ into 24/7 electricity with efficiencies of 10-15 percent. For shallower wells (100– $1 2 0 ~ ^ { \\circ } \\mathrm { C } )$ , closed-loop heat can still drive district heating, lithium brine pre-heating, or on-site desalination creating stacked revenue streams even when electric levelized cost of energy (LCOE) is marginal.

By marrying proven oil-field hardware with next-generation heat-extraction methods, closed-loop geothermal retrofits offer Texas a practical bridge from its hydrocarbon past to a resilient, carbon-free energy future.

# GEOTHERMAL ENERGY STORAGE

While geothermal energy is traditionally viewed as a baseload resource, emerging technologies now enable its use as a thermal energy storage medium. This new form of long-duration energy storage complements both renewable and dispatchable generation. By circulating working fluid through closed-loop systems during periods of excess wind or solar generation, heat can be stored in subsurface formations and released later to produce power during peak demand or low-renewable intervals.

This “charge–discharge” capability transforms geothermal reservoirs into thermal batteries with multi-day duration and negligible degradation. This new method of storing excess energy through batteries will provide a needed relief for both operators of large energy users like data centers, and manufacturers, who are looking for opportunities to reduce ERCOT grid strain and ensuring that the PUCT isn’t passing these energy user’s costs onto consumers.

# ECONOMIC OPPROTUNTIY

ERCOT’s rapid influx of solar and wind has lowered marginal energy prices but increased the demand for firm, clean capacity that can respond when the wind calms or the sun sets. Retrofitted geothermal wells deliver exactly that attribute.

Unlike gas peakers, they require no fuel deliveries or hedging, and unlike batteries, their output does not degrade over a multi-day weather event. For rural counties wrestling with orphan-well liabilities, the conversion offers dual relief: avoided plugging expenses and a new taxable generation asset.

At the workforce level, the overlap between drilling, completions, and geothermal retrofit work is increasingly similar, allowing floorhands, wireline crews, and rig hands to pivot into longlived clean-energy jobs with minimal retraining.

The opportunity is not theoretical; venture-backed firms have already secured PPAs in Nevadavii providing real-world data points for Texas legislators and investors to benchmark.

# POLICY GAPS

Despite technical feasibility, market uptake in Texas is stalled by a regulatory framework built for either hydrocarbon production or traditional geothermal, with no streamlined lane for retrofit wells. The Railroad Commission’s plugging rules make no distinction between a well destined for cement and a well poised for conversion, locking operators into a liability mindset rather than a redevelopment mindset. Meanwhile, county tax appraisals often spike the moment a renewable project is announced, undercutting project finance.

On the grid side, ERCOT still lacks a standardized small-generator interconnection pathway for sub5 MW geothermal units, forcing project developers into bespoke negotiations that add cost and delay.

Finally, bonding and royalty structures do not yet reward methane-leak avoidance or groundwater protection—two public benefits that closed-loop retrofits inherently deliver.

Without targeted statutory tweaks and clear agency guidance, the default outcome is to pay $$ 35 k\\mathrm { ~ - ~ } $ 100 k$ per well to abandon a resource that could produce revenue and resilience for decades.

1. No asset-screening tool to identify geothermalready wells.

2. Bonding rules treat retrofit and abandonment identically, removing economic incentive.

3. The Permitting path unclear: geothermal retrofits would be regulated under multiple, sometimes conflicting, statutes.

4. Lack of standardized Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) structure for small-scale baseload supply into ERCOT.

# RECOMMENDATIONS FOR LAWMAKERS

1. Create a “Geothermal-Ready Well Registry.” Texas Railroad Commission (RRC) and UT Bureau of Economic Geology compile depth, casing integrity, and formation temperature data.

# 2) Establish Bond-for-Retrofit Credits.

Allow operators to apply up to $80 %$ of plugging bond toward retrofit CAPEX if the well is converted within 24 months. Eventually, the legislature should create a “retrofit fund” that allows complete mitigation of zombie wells.

# 3) One-Stop Permit Desk.

Authorize RRC to issue a consolidated geothermal retrofit permit within 90 days, coordinating TCEQ and surface-owner approvals.

4. Pilot-Project Tax Incentive.

Five-year ad-valorem tax abatement for the first MWs of closed-loop capacity brought online if connected to ERCOT grid.

5. Standardized 10-Year Baseload PPA Template. ERCOT and PUC provide an off-the-shelf contract for utilities purchasing output from retrofitted wells ≤5 MW.
